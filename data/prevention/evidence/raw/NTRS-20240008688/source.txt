                                                      Apollo-Era Historical “Scratch and Poke” Tests

     •     After the Apollo I incident, more extensive
           testing of materials used in the suit volume
           was performed

     •     The “scratch and poke” test had a flat
           electrode plate and material sample was
           placed in a 100% oxygen chamber

     •     A needle electrode was then used to scratch
           and poke the material, creating shorts to the
           flat plate through the material in an attempt to
           create an ignition

     •     A material was determined to be “safe” if no
           ignition occurred during 15 minutes of arcing
                                                                                            Scratch and Poke Test
           across the frayed sample                                                         Diagram and Test Photo

     •     A steady-state current limit of 500mA was then
           used broadly across the suit programs (Space
           Shuttle Program (SSP) and ISS EMU)

                                                                    2005-Era “Scratch and Poke” Tests

     •     SSP Mission STS-113, after completion of EVA 2, EVA Crewmember 1 (EV1)
           reported that the biomedical cable in his ISS EMU had broken wire insulation
           with wires protruding
     •     Test effort was initiated with the Johnson Space Center (JSC) - White Sands
           Test Facility (WSTF) to characterize current EMU materials and quantify the
           risk for bio/comm cables
     •     The team developed different qualitative screening methods to evaluate
           materials for flammability in this application: “scratch” test; “poke” test
            • “Scratch” testing dragged a stylus across the material while arcing                   Scratch Test (top) and Poke Test (bottom)
                through the material
            • “Poke” testing was similar to the Apollo era testing performed that poked
                a small stylus into the material, arcing through the material
     •     During Scratch/Poke testing, both tests have an arc occurring at one location
           perhaps multiple times sequentially, and lower-ignition currents are measured,
           indicating that it is more severe to arc in multiple locations
     •     With the arc-to-arc, test-to-test variation of these two methods, the team
           focused on a more repeatable method that became the NASA-STD-6001
           A.2.11.6 method

                                                                                            Summary
            • The wire-break ignition test was developed after re-creation and review of the Apollo era tests that
              employed scratch and poke electrode techniques to devise a more repeatable and conservative test
              that still represents a system failure mode.

            • A body of data was generated for materials characterization considering thermal decomposition into a
              combustible vapor with subsequent ignition from an arc discharge.

            • NASA-STD-6001 A.2.11.6 wire break ignition methodology is inadequate for characterizing the
              ignition hazard for system design as it provides a single open-circuit voltage that does not capture the
              work required to ignite bulk material. (F-1)

            • NASA is conducting an internal development effort to define an alternate energy approach and will
              refine the current test methodology that will result in a replacement wire ignition test method. (R-1).


            This snapshot of this effort is being presented at this International Conference on Environmental
            Systems (ICES) in July 2024. An in-depth report encompassing all of this effort is in work and
            will be going through the NASA Engineering and Safety Center (NESC) Peer Review process.

ICES-2024-476, Wire-break Ignition Testing of Materials for Spacesuit Fire Hazard Control
                                                        Test Setup Parameters Altering the Test Setup

     • Wire placement:
         • The wire must be in direct contact with the sample to obtain ignition.
         • Occasionally, the wire would lift off of the surface of the material when heated.
         • High-speed video has to be reviewed from each run to ensure that the wire remained in
              contact with the sample.
     • Wire length:
         • An increase in the wire length lowered the ignition energy, as this resulted in transmission
              energy loss with less delivered to the sample as measured at the contacts.
     • Wire gauge/handling to avoid breakage:
                                                                                                                       Pyropel with 38 Gauge Wire
         • As the wire gauge increases and the resulting cross-section decreases, it becomes much
              more difficult to handle and secure the wire without damage.
    • Ability to control wire-breakage location in small-gauge wires:
        • With the heat sinking at the terminals, the wire has peak heating at the center of the U-loop. However, peak heating can occur
             elsewhere if the wire is damaged or altered.
    • Heat flux/thermal conductivity of the materials under test:
        • If the material sample is highly conductive, it can dissipate heat away from the localized heating zone, with the wire sinking it to the
             copper baseplate. This produces a higher apparent ignition energy, skewing the results.
    • Combustible vapors:
        • The combustible vapors must be accumulated above the lower flammability limit and below the upper flammability limit. This time
             component of the test forces iteration to find the optimal ignition point.
        • The material density in proximity to the wire also contributes to how much of the heat-affected zone will experience thermal
             decomposition and potentially source combustible vapors. The Figure shows the rather small size of the heat-affected zone along
             with the open fiber density of the Pyropel leading to less direct contact with the given material.

                                                                         Test Setup Altering Parameters

        •    Potential improvements under active investigation include refining or evolving the existing wire-break method with
             changes such as:
               • Programmed current (A) ramp rate
               • Re-test “sweet spot” (seconds) to maximum ignition frequency
               • Add spark plug for more repeatable arc ignition
               • Instant calculation of integrated I^2t, current at break, voltage at break, power at break, and integrated energy
                 (Joules) so operator knows step-down was accomplished
               • Measure heat flux of ignited smoke

        •    Additional considerations include the splitting of the different functions performed by the current wire-break setup:
              1. Perform a materials characterization prior to the test using thermos gravimetric analysis (TGA), differential
                  scanning calorimetry (DSC), and gas chromatograph/mass spectrometer (GC/MS) to determine when thermal
                  decomposition occurs for the given material and what combustible vapor is produced
              2. Use the setup-controlled preheat to generate the combustible vapor via thermal decomposition across a larger
                  area of the sample, addressing the small heat-affected zone issue
              3. Use a controlled igniter similar to a spark plug to generate a repeatable and controlled spark with variable energy
                  for the selected test condition
